## ASSISTANT
The comic needs more than “old fisherman catches fish.” It needs a **repeatable engine**—a reason something goes wrong every day, even when he is doing almost nothing.

My strongest concept is:

## **The Lake Knows Better**

The old fisherman believes he understands the lake better than anyone alive.

The lake seems determined to prove otherwise.

He treats every ordinary event like an ancient sign: wind direction, birds, lily pads, insects, clouds, ripples, strange noises. Sometimes he is completely wrong. Sometimes he is accidentally right. The comedy comes from his absolute confidence while nature, technology, animals, and younger fishermen quietly undermine him.

The ongoing conflict becomes:

**Old-school wisdom versus a world that refuses to cooperate.**

He is not incompetent. He is actually pretty good. He is just stubborn, dramatic, and incapable of admitting that luck, gadgets, or anyone else helped.

A typical strip could be:

1. He announces a fishing rule.
2. Someone or something challenges it.
3. Chaos follows.
4. He reframes the result as proof he was right.

Example:

**Panel 1:** 
“The bass won’t bite before the fog lifts.”

**Panel 2:** 
A fish jumps directly into his kayak.

**Panel 3:** 
He calmly removes the hook while everyone stares.

**Panel 4:** 
“Fog lifted under the boat.”

That structure can run for a very long time.

## Recurring cast

A daily strip gets much stronger when the fisherman has people and creatures to bounce off.

### **The Old Fisherman**
Possible names: **Old Man Timber**, **Captain Mudwater**, **Uncle Bass**, **Buck Reed**, or **Hank Waters**.

He is confident, philosophical, mildly ridiculous, and almost impossible to embarrass.

His central flaw:

**He would rather invent a new law of fishing than admit he was wrong.**

### **The Fish**

A recurring bass that keeps escaping him.

The fisherman may call it:

- Big Martha
- Old Bucketmouth
- The General
- Walter
- The Green Devil

The fish becomes his nemesis. Readers occasionally see what the fish is doing underwater, but the fisherman does not.

The fish could deliberately steal bait, move his bobber, lead him into weeds, or appear behind him whenever he declares the lake empty.

### **The Young Gadget Fisherman**

A younger fisherman with sonar, GPS, weather apps, cameras, sensors, and expensive equipment.

He has all the information but none of the instinct.

The old fisherman mocks the technology, then secretly uses it when nobody is looking.

Their rivalry provides endless material:

> “That screen can’t tell you what a fish is thinking.”

> “It says there are thirty-two fish beneath you.”

> “They’re discussing me.”

### **The Game Warden**

The only person who can make him nervous.

The fisherman always appears suspicious even when he has done nothing wrong.

The warden sees a pirate flag, six rods, homemade equipment, strange bait, and a kayak held together with stickers and assumes something illegal is happening.

Usually, the actual violation belongs to someone else.

### **The Duck**

A shameless duck that steals bait, food, tools, and occasionally the fish.

The fisherman treats it like a criminal mastermind.

The duck never speaks. It does not need to.

### **The Voice at Home**

A spouse, friend, sibling, or neighbor who never appears fully on-screen at first.

They hear his heroic version of the day, while the strip shows what actually happened.

He says:

> “Had to fight off a monster.”

The image shows him wrestling a tangled branch.

This could become a recurring closing gag.

# Strong recurring conflicts

These give you daily plots without needing a completely new premise each time.

## **Man versus Fish**

The legendary bass repeatedly outsmarts him.

He begins treating the pursuit like a personal war, while the fish treats it like casual entertainment.

## **Tradition versus Technology**

He claims electronics ruin fishing.

Meanwhile, he has a motorized kayak, sonar, batteries, rod holders, GPS, a phone, and probably three cameras.

His definition of “traditional fishing” changes whenever convenient.

## **Man versus Nature**

Wind, mud, lily pads, mosquitoes, heat, rain, birds, turtles, beavers, and floating logs all become opponents.

Nature is never malicious. It just refuses to respect his dignity.

## **Man versus Pride**

This is the biggest one.

He cannot admit:

- he forgot the bait,
- he lost the fish,
- he fell in,
- the duck won,
- the kid caught the bigger fish,
- the expensive gadget worked,
- or he has been fishing in the wrong spot all morning.

His explanations become more elaborate than the original failure.

## **Legend versus Reality**

The story he tells afterward is always bigger than what happened.

This could be your signature format:

**First three panels:** What actually happened. 
**Fourth panel:** His version of the story.

# Recurring strip formats

Rotating formats will keep the comic from feeling repetitive.

## **Rule of the Day**

Every strip begins with him sharing one of his “ancient fishing rules.”

Examples:

- Never trust calm water.
- Fish can smell desperation.
- A quiet fisherman catches more fish.
- Never change bait after blaming the weather.
- The bigger the splash, the smaller the witness list.

The rule is tested before the end.

## **The One That Got Away**

He almost catches the recurring legendary bass.

Each failure reveals more of the fish’s personality.

## **Dock Philosophy**

Someone asks him a simple question.

He gives a profound fishing metaphor that becomes increasingly confusing.

> “How do you know when to let go?”

> “Depends. Are we talking about love, regret, or a twelve-pound fluorocarbon line?”

## **Underwater Cutaway**

One panel shows the fish reacting to what he is doing above.

This creates jokes the fisherman never sees.

He carefully selects a realistic lure.

Underwater, the fish are eating a discarded cheese puff.

## **The Story Back Home**

He narrates the day as an epic adventure.

The visuals quietly contradict every sentence.

## **Fishing Emergency**

A tiny inconvenience is treated like a military crisis:

- dropped sandwich,
- dead battery,
- tangled line,
- missing sunglasses,
- duck near the bait,
- coffee tipped over,
- phone dropped somewhere in the kayak.

# The best overall premise

I would frame the series as:

## **Old Man of the Lake**

Every morning, a stubborn old fisherman paddles onto the lake convinced he has finally figured it out.

Every morning, the lake teaches him something else.

He battles a legendary bass, modern technology, wildlife, weather, visitors, and his own pride—then returns home with a fish, an excuse, or a story that improves every time he tells it.

That gives you:

- an emotional center,
- a daily conflict,
- a recognizable world,
- recurring characters,
- and a consistent punchline engine.

The comic should affectionately tease him without making him a fool. The reader should think:

**He is ridiculous—but I would absolutely go fishing with him.**
